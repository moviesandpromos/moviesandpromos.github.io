Topic 1

How a strategy works

Every strategy has two halves: a rule for when to enter a trade, and a rule for when to exit it. Together they define the entire behavior.

An entry rule is a condition. It says "buy when the RSI drops below 30" or "buy when the 50-day average is rising and price is above it." When the condition is true on a given day, the strategy enters a position at the next day's open.

An exit rule is another set of conditions. It could be "sell when RSI rises above 70," or "sell when the price has risen 15%," or "sell if the price drops 8%." Whatever the specific wording, the exit rule is what closes the position and locks in the profit or loss.

The strategies below are standard templates. StockWise uses them because they're well-known, easy to reason about, and each captures a different market behavior.

Important: the same strategy can be profitable on one stock and unprofitable on another. That's exactly what walk-forward analysis reveals — whether a strategy's historical profits come from a real pattern or from luck.

Strategy A

Trend Following

Ride an established uptrend until it shows signs of ending.

Strategy A Trend Following

Buy strength, hold while the trend is intact, exit when momentum fades.

Entry rules

  • Trend = UP (price above the 50-day moving average)
  • RSI > 50 (momentum is positive)
  • MACD bullish (short-term average above long-term)

Exit rules

  • Hard stop: -5%
  • Trend no longer UP (price drops below the moving average)
  • MACD turns bearish AND RSI falls below 50
Worked example — Reliance

Imagine Reliance is trading at ₹2,400 in January 2023 and all three entry conditions are true: the 50-day average is rising, RSI is 58, and MACD has just turned bullish.

The strategy stayed in the trade for five months and exited when the trend flipped. It captured most of the move — not the exact top, but most of the rise.

Good at

Capturing big sustained moves. In a strong bull market, this strategy often beats buy-and-hold because it exits before big declines.

Struggles with

Choppy sideways markets. Frequent false signals can cause many small losses that add up.

Strategy B

Oversold Reversal

Buy when a stock has fallen too far, too fast — expecting a bounce.

Strategy B Oversold Reversal

Buy weakness, exit on a fixed target or stop, hold for a short period.

Entry rules

  • RSI crosses below 30 (oversold territory)
  • MACD histogram rising off the bottom (early momentum recovery)

Exit rules

  • Profit target: +5%
  • Stop loss: -3%
  • Time stop: 15 days if neither target nor stop is hit
Worked example — HDFC Bank

HDFC Bank has fallen 8% in two weeks on no news. RSI is 27 and the MACD histogram has just started rising. All entry conditions are met.

Entry
₹1,500
10 Mar 2024
Day 4
₹1,530
+2% — bounce starting
Day 9
₹1,575
+5% target hit — exit

The trade lasted nine days and closed at +5%. In a losing scenario, a stop at -3% would exit the position before a larger drawdown developed.

Good at

Range-bound markets. When a stock oscillates between support and resistance, buying near support and selling near resistance works repeatedly.

Struggles with

Downtrends. In a sustained decline, "oversold" keeps getting more oversold — every dip-buy hits the stop. This is why the -3% stop matters.

Strategy C

Momentum Recovery

Buy after a decline, but only once momentum has clearly started to recover.

Strategy C Momentum Recovery

Wait for the reversal to confirm, then enter alongside it.

Entry rules

  • Trend = DOWN or FLAT (the decline already happened)
  • RSI crosses above 40 (momentum turning up)
  • MACD bullish (confirmation from a second indicator)

Exit rules

  • Hard stop: -5%
  • Trend no longer UP
  • MACD turns bearish AND RSI falls below 50
Worked example — Infosys

Infosys has been in a downtrend for weeks. Then in early August 2023, RSI crosses above 40, MACD turns bullish, and the trend flips to UP. The reversal is confirmed.

The difference from Strategy B is the confirmation: Strategy B acts on the reversal beginning, Strategy C waits for it to be visible in two indicators. The trade-off is a slightly later entry but fewer failed signals.

Good at

V-shaped recoveries. When a stock bottoms and starts a new leg up, this strategy catches most of the move without being whipsawed.

Struggles with

False bottoms. Sometimes what looks like a reversal fails and the decline continues. The -5% stop is what limits the damage.

Strategy D

Current Levels (custom)

Build your own entry rules by combining the 11 indicators StockWise tracks — and pick your own exit mode.

Strategy D Current Levels

Full customisation: entry thresholds you set, plus one of three exit modes.

Entry rules (example configuration)

  • Trend = ANY (no filter)
  • RSI < 40
  • MACD state = ANY (no filter)

Exit modes

  • Bounce — fixed target, fixed stop, and a time limit
  • Momentum — trend + MACD reversal, plus an ATR-based trailing stop
  • Trailing — follow the price up using an ATR-based stop that moves with the peak
Worked example — Nifty 50 with Bounce mode

You configure entry as "Trend ANY + RSI < 40" and exit as "Bounce: target 5%, stop 3%, max hold 15 days."

Entry
18,200
RSI = 38 → signal fires
Day 6
18,550
+1.9%
Day 11
19,110
+5% target hit — exit

With Bounce mode, the trade exits the moment any of the three exit conditions is met — target, stop, or time limit. This gives a clean, predictable holding period.

The exit mode you choose changes the strategy's character. Bounce is best for range-bound markets. Momentum is best when you expect a sustained move. Trailing is best when you want to capture a big trend but protect gains.

Topic 6

Building your own strategy

The four templates above are starting points. Any of them can be combined, simplified, or built from scratch using the same building blocks.

A strategy is just a list of entry conditions and a list of exit conditions. The condition types are the same regardless of the strategy — indicators like RSI, MACD, DMA trend, and Bollinger Bands.

The full menu of conditions StockWise supports for stocks:

Condition Types of rule Common use
RSI > 70, < 30, crosses above/below Momentum & oversold signals
DMA Trend UP, DOWN, SIDEWAYS Overall direction filter
MACD Positive cross, negative cross, unchanged Momentum confirmation
Bollinger Bands Near upper, near lower, within range Mean reversion entries
Strategy Signal Trend momentum, early momentum, reversal Combined multi-indicator signal
Breakout YES / NO Price breaking a range
OBV Rising, falling, stable Volume confirmation
VWAP Bullish, bearish, strongly so Intraday trend context
ADX Strong / weak trend, directional Trend strength filter
Stochastic Oversold, overbought, momentum Short-term reversal

For indices, the vocabulary is smaller but the idea is the same — trend direction, RSI level, MACD cross. Indices don't have earnings or volume spikes, so volume-based conditions don't apply.

A note on building: adding more conditions usually makes a strategy look better in a backtest but perform worse in practice. Simple strategies (2 or 3 entry conditions) tend to survive walk-forward and Monte Carlo analysis better than complex ones. If your strategy has 6 conditions, try removing half and see if the results hold up.

Whatever you build, the two reports tell you whether it holds up:

  • Walk-forward analysis — does the strategy keep working on data it was never trained on?
  • Monte Carlo analysis — given the trades it does make, how bad could the outcomes get?

A strategy that passes both is worth taking seriously. A strategy that fails either is a hypothesis, not a system.
